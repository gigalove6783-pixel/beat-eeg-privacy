Does a brain–computer interface reveal who you are?

A BCI turns imagined left- or right-hand movement into commands and often sends something off the device: a feature vector, a command, or both. We measured how well an eavesdropper can tell which of 9 people produced what was sent, and whether that can be reduced without hurting control. A sealed replication then re-tested the main claims on 54 people.

Identity score = balanced accuracy of an attacker naming the right person. Chance is – for 9 users and – for 54; 1.00 means everyone is identified.

Every result carries its evidence level: Confirmatory = pre-registered and sealed before the held-out data were scored; Exploratory = fixed before its own run but hypothesis-generating. Hover or focus a number to see its source file.

What this does not show

  • A 9-person closed set (BCI Competition IV 2a), not a population.
  • Arm control is a replay of recorded EEG, not closed-loop control with a person.
  • An attacker's failure is not anonymization.
  • Device compromise, identifying metadata and unobserved sensitive attributes are out of scope.
  • Replay processing time is not online latency.

The study at a glance

Seven experiments on one question. A–G use the 9 users of BCI Competition IV 2a; L repeats the key claims on 54 users. The attacker sees only what the device sends, trains its own classifiers and names the user; it never sees raw EEG or account metadata.

    Experiment C

    Replay: steer the arm, watch the eavesdropper

    Recorded session-E trials drive a one-joint arm: left −5°, right +5°, hold when the decoder's confidence is below τ. One episode is 20 trials. Meanwhile an independently trained attacker sees only what is transmitted and guesses who the user is from each group of k consecutive outputs.       Experiment C

      Commands accumulate identity

      Sending only left/right/hold leaks about an order of magnitude less per message than sending features, but it does not vanish: identity leakage generally grows the longer an eavesdropper watches the command stream, though not monotonically.

      Experiment G

      Pitfall 2: a published defence, re-identified with a few labels

      A training-free defence published for this dataset is fitted on a reference session and evaluated with attackers trained on that session. We re-implemented it, reproduced its result, then gave the attacker what a real service may hold: a few labelled trials per user from the deployment period.
